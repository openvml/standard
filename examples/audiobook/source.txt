The Lantern Keeper and the Lost Bell
=====================================

Old Jonas tended the lighthouse at Black Point for forty years, high above a
town that had almost forgotten the sea. Every evening at dusk he climbed the
iron stairs, lit the great lamp, and let its beam sweep the dark water like a
long, slow question.

One autumn night, as the wind rose and the rain set in, a small figure knocked
on the keeper's door. It was twelve-year-old Marta, drenched to the skin,
clutching a copper bell that had come loose from the harbor chapel.

"Jonas," she said, breathless, "the bell fell during the storm. Without it,
the ships can't hear us through the fog."

The old man studied her for a long moment, then shook his head. "That bell
isn't mine to ring," he said gently. "I keep the light, child. The captain of
the Morning Star is the only one who decides what the harbor hears."

"Then let the light speak for it," Marta argued, setting the bell on the table
with a soft, flat clang. "The beam reaches farther than any voice."

From the doorway came a low chuckle. Eli, the young fisherman who owned the
Morning Star, leaned against the frame, shaking rain from his boots.

"She's got a point, Jonas," Eli said. "That lamp has brought a hundred boats
home. A bell that rings on the shore can't do what your light does on the sea."

Jonas sighed and looked out the window at the churning black water. "Light is
for guiding," he rumbled. "Bell is for warning. Both matter, if you know when
to use them."

Marta picked up her bell and hurried to the chapel. Jonas trimmed his lamp and
returned to his watch. It was not long before the fog closed in, thick and
white, and the shore bell began to ring — steady, patient, over and over.

In the morning, the Morning Star came home through the mist, her sails crisp
with dew, her deck bright with lantern-light. And the old keeper understood at
last that a keeper need not be the one who rings. He needed only to tend the
flame that made the ringing matter.

That night, Jonas hung the copper bell at the foot of the lighthouse stairs —
a small, gleaming promise — and went up to light the lamp, as he had every
night for forty years.
